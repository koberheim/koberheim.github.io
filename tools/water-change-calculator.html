IVMethod & limits

The math is straight dilution: new nitrate = current × (1 − change fraction) + tap × change fraction. Solved for the change fraction needed to hit a target, that's (current − target) ÷ (current − tap). With 0 ppm tap water this reduces to the simpler version most guides show — current 40, target 20, is exactly a 50% change — but it stays correct when your source water isn't nitrate-free.

This calculator only models dilution, not the nitrate your bioload will keep adding after the change, or how fast plants and water changes together bring a number down over days. It answers "how much water, right now, to hit this number" — not "will my levels stay there." If nitrate climbs straight back up in a day or two, that's a bioload/filtration/feeding question, not a water-change one.

A note on ammonia and nitrite instead of nitrate: the same dilution math applies, but if either of those is reading above 0 in a tank with fish in it, that's usually an active emergency (an uncycled or crashed filter), not a routine maintenance question — do the largest safe water change now and figure out the cause, rather than planning a gentle multi-week schedule.

VCommon questions

Why doesn't two 25% changes equal one 50%?

Because the second change dilutes what's already-diluted water, not the original concentration. From 40 ppm: one 25% change gives 40×0.75 = 30 ppm. A second 25% change gives 30×0.75 = 22.5 ppm — not 20 ppm, which is what a single 50% change would give (40×0.5). Each repeated change removes a smaller absolute amount, which is exactly why the schedule table above matters more than a single ratio.

Is a big single water change dangerous?

The nitrate math itself doesn't care how large the change is, but the fish do — a large, sudden shift in temperature, pH, or hardness is the actual risk, not the water-change size directly. Match new water's temperature closely, and if you're not dealing with a dangerous ammonia/nitrite spike, a 40%+ change is usually fine done carefully; going gentler over a couple of changes is the safer default if you're unsure of your tap water's chemistry relative to your tank's.

My tap water nitrate is higher than my target — now what?

Water changes alone can't get you below your tap water's own nitrate level, no matter how much you change — you're diluting toward that floor, not toward zero. Options are a nitrate-reducing filter media, blending in RO/DI water to lower the effective source nitrate, or accepting a higher target if the level isn't otherwise a problem for your stock.
