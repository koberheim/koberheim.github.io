IVMethod & limits

This calculator uses the surface-area rule — roughly 1″ of slim-bodied fish per 12 sq. in. of water surface for a standard, well-filtered tank — instead of the widely-repeated but inaccurate “1 inch per gallon” rule, which ignores oxygen exchange, filtration and tank shape entirely. On top of that base budget, each species carries a rough bioload weight so that heavier waste-producers (like plecos or goldfish) count for more than a same-length tetra.

Bioload isn't the only thing that matters, so the calculator also checks your list against each species' minimum tank volume and length (a fish that outgrows the tank is a problem no filter solves), whether every species shares a workable temperature range, and a set of well-documented conflicts: fin-nippers with long-finned fish, adult predators with bite-sized tankmates, shrimp with fish that hunt them, territorial species competing for the same space, multiple male bettas, and shoaling fish kept in groups too small to feel secure.

This is a planning estimate, not a guarantee, and it is not a complete compatibility matrix. Species figures are typical adult values from standard care-sheet ranges — individual fish, strains and setups vary. Always research the adult size, temperament and specific needs of any species before you buy, cycle every new tank fully, and go slower than the calculator allows if you're new to the hobby. Coverage is roughly 70 of the most commonly kept freshwater species and invertebrates, chosen for accuracy over raw count — it doesn't cover saltwater, or most specialist and wild-caught species.

VCommon questions

Is the “1 inch of fish per gallon” rule wrong?

It's incomplete rather than flat-out wrong — it was never a bad starting point for small tetras in a small tank, but it ignores tank shape, filtration, and how heavily different species pollute the water. A tall, narrow 20 gallon holds noticeably fewer fish safely than a short, wide 20 gallon, and a single goldfish produces far more waste than an inch of neon tetra. See our surface-area rule guide for the longer explanation.

What counts as "overstocked"?

There's no single hard line — it's a spectrum of risk. Above roughly 100% of the budget this calculator estimates, ammonia and nitrite can build up faster than your filter and water changes can process it, especially in a newer, not-fully-cycled tank. Going a little over isn't automatically dangerous if you're doing frequent water changes and have strong filtration; going a lot over usually is.

Does a planted tank really let me keep more fish?

Somewhat, yes — live plants use ammonia/nitrate as a nutrient and can add some daytime oxygen, so a heavily (not lightly) planted tank gets a modest bump in this calculator. It's not a substitute for filtration or water changes, and a few plants scattered around a tank won't move the number much.

Why isn't my fish in the species list?

The list covers roughly 70 of the most commonly kept freshwater species and invertebrates — deliberately curated rather than exhaustive, because hand-checked data is more useful than a big list of rough guesses. It doesn't cover saltwater or most specialist and wild-caught species; for those, a larger database like AqAdvisor has more coverage. Missing something common? Suggest it on GitHub.

My fish is small at the store — why does it say I need a huge tank?

Because pet shops sell juveniles. Common plecos are sold at 2″ and reach roughly 18″; clown loaches and bala sharks are sold at an inch or two and reach a foot; comet goldfish are pond fish. These are the most common expensive mistakes in the hobby, which is why the calculator flags adult size rather than the size you'd bring home.

Do I need to cycle my tank first?

Yes — and this calculator assumes you have. A fully cycled tank has enough beneficial bacteria to convert fish waste (ammonia) into less harmful nitrate. Adding a full stocking list to a brand-new tank will cause an ammonia spike regardless of what any calculator says. Cycle first, then add fish gradually, testing as you go.
