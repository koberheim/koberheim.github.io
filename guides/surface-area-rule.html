The surface-area rule, explained

Why "1 inch of fish per gallon" isn't the full picture, and what to use instead.

If you've spent any time researching how many fish you can keep, you've run into “1 inch of fish per gallon of water.” It's easy to remember and easy to apply — which is exactly why it spread so widely, and exactly why it causes so many overstocked tanks.

Where the rule came from, and why it's incomplete

The inch-per-gallon rule is a rough proxy for a real thing: how much waste a tank's biology can safely process before ammonia and nitrite build up. But it uses volume as the stand-in, when the thing that actually limits a tank — oxygen exchange and, downstream of that, how efficiently beneficial bacteria can process waste — happens mostly at the water's surface, not throughout its volume.

That's why two tanks with the same gallon count can safely hold very different amounts of fish. A 20-gallon "long" tank (30″ × 12″ footprint, 12″ tall) has about 360 square inches of surface. A 20-gallon "high" tank, closer to 24″ × 12″ but 16″ tall, has only around 288 square inches — a fifth less surface for the same volume, and gas exchange doesn't care how tall the tank is.

The rule also treats every inch of fish as equal, which isn't true either: a 5-inch bristlenose pleco produces a lot more bioload than 5 inches of neon tetra, because it eats and excretes more relative to its body length. And it says nothing about filtration — a tank with a properly sized filter and regular water changes can support more fish than the same tank with a weak one.

The surface-area rule

A better rule of thumb, widely used by more experienced fishkeepers: roughly 1 inch of slim-bodied fish per 12 square inches of water surface, for a standard, properly filtered community tank. Scale that divisor down (more fish per square inch) for stronger filtration and frequent water changes, or up (fewer fish) for light filtration.

Worked example: a standard 20-gallon long tank (30″ × 12″ = 360 sq. in.) with standard filtration gives a budget of 360 ÷ 12 = 30 inches of slim-bodied fish. Six neon tetras (1.5″ each) and six harlequin rasboras (2″ each) come to 6×1.5 + 6×2 = 21 raw inches of fish — comfortably under budget, with room for a small cleanup crew.

One clarification, because it trips people up: those 21 inches are raw body length. The calculator goes a step further and weights each species by how much waste it actually produces, so the same stock comes to about 10.5 adjusted inches — small tetras and rasboras are cleaner than average per inch. A messier fish moves the number the other way: a single 5″ bristlenose pleco is 5 raw inches but 7.5 adjusted. Raw inches tell you about swimming space; adjusted inches tell you about your filter's workload.

It's still a rule of thumb, not physics. It doesn't replace testing your water, cycling a new tank fully, or researching the specific species you want. But it accounts for the two biggest things the inch-per-gallon rule ignores — tank shape and filtration — which is why it tends to give a much more realistic answer.
